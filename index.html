Impact on Maritime Infrastructure · Ports & Fleet

Session 84 of the Marine Environment Protection Committee

MEPC 84 ran from 27 April to 1 May 2026 in London

The main takeaway is that the Net-Zero Framework survived, despite pressure from the United States, Saudi Arabia, the UAE and other countries seeking to reopen or modify key parts of the framework.

That matters because the debate is no longer only about climate ambition. It is about whether shipowners, ports and investors receive a credible regulatory signal — or remain stuck waiting for a framework that still lacks full political alignment.

Regulatory Framework

What Is the Net-Zero Framework?

The NZF is the IMO's global mechanism to reduce GHG emissions from maritime transport and move the sector toward climate neutrality before 2050.

Carbon Pricing

Highest disagreement

A price on carbon to incentivize the transition to alternative fuels across the global merchant fleet.

Fuel Standard

Partial disagreement

A progressive reduction in the carbon intensity of marine fuel through a global IMO standard.

Green Fund

Linked to pricing debate

Carbon pricing revenues directed to transition infrastructure and support for developing countries.

The lack of adoption has a double downstream impact: it delays investment in ships because owners cannot choose a future-proof fuel pathway, and it delays investment in ports because terminals cannot justify which bunkering and electrification infrastructure to build.

Impact · Global Fleet

Uncertainty Paralyses Shipyards

Shrinking Orderbook

Not knowing which fuel will dominate — LNG, methanol, ammonia or hydrogen — shipowners delay newbuilding decisions. Shipyard order books contract.

Ageing Fleet

Slower newbuilding rates raise the average age of the global fleet. Older vessels mean higher energy consumption, more emissions and greater operating costs.

Fragmented Capital

Banks and investors demand higher risk premiums when they cannot assess which technology will prevail. More expensive financing, less profitable projects.

No Economies of Scale

Multiple propulsion technologies developed simultaneously prevent any single one from reaching critical mass. Unit costs remain elevated across the board.

Impact · Port Infrastructure

Ports Cannot Plan Without a Clear Signal

A port investing today in bunkering infrastructure needs to know which fuel will dominate by 2035. Without an NZF, that calculation is impossible.

Bunkering on Hold

Alternative fuel terminals (ammonia, methanol, H₂) remain on standby or in indefinite pilot phase.

Shore Power Stalled

OPS / cold ironing electrification projects lose budget priority amid regulatory uncertainty.

Duplicated CAPEX

Without technological consensus, ports spread capital across multiple infrastructure types — raising costs without efficiency gains.

Rotterdam, Singapore and the world's major hubs all have energy transition plans that depend directly on the IMO defining the fuel of the future.

The Paradox

Diversification Without Direction ≠ Strategy

Without a clear NZF, the sector does not converge on a solution, it disperses. This is not strategic diversification. It is uncertainty disguised as optionality.

Without NZF

  • — Capital fragmented across multiple technologies
  • — Port infrastructure duplicated or underutilised
  • — Shipyards with no critical mass in any propulsion
  • — Higher risk premium demanded by financiers

With NZF Adopted

  • — Clear regulatory signal for owners and investors
  • — Technology convergence and economies of scale
  • — Ports can plan infrastructure on a 10-year horizon
  • — Reduced financial risk premium across the sector
